<h2 id="what-is">What Is Contribution Margin?</h2>
<p>Contribution margin is the money left from a sale after you pay the costs that rise and fall with that sale. Those are your <strong>variable costs</strong>: materials, labor on the job, shipping, payment fees, sales commissions, and, for a marketing campaign, the cost of winning the customer.</p>
<p>What is left &ldquo;contributes&rdquo; toward your <strong>fixed costs</strong>, like rent, salaries, and software, which you pay no matter how much you sell. Once fixed costs are covered, every extra dollar of contribution margin is profit. <a href="https://www.investopedia.com/terms/c/contributionmargin.asp" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Investopedia</a> and the standard <a href="https://courses.lumenlearning.com/wm-managerialaccounting/chapter/contribution-margin/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">managerial accounting texts</a> define it the same way.</p>
<p>That is why finance teams love it. Revenue tells you how big a sale was. Contribution margin tells you whether it was worth making. (For the history and variations of the term, see <a href="https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Contribution_margin" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Wikipedia&rsquo;s entry on contribution margin</a>.)</p>
<p>Before you calculate contribution margin, sort every cost into one of two buckets: does it go up when you sell more (variable), or would you pay it anyway (fixed)? Getting that split right matters more than any formula.</p>

<h2 id="formulas">The Contribution Margin Formulas</h2>
<p>You can calculate contribution margin three ways, and you will use all three. Each one answers a slightly different question.</p>
<div class="ccm-formula">
  <span class="ccm-f-label">Total contribution margin</span>
  <span class="ccm-f-main">Contribution Margin = Revenue &minus; Variable Costs</span>
  <span class="ccm-f-label">Contribution margin per unit</span>
  CM per Unit = Selling Price per Unit &minus; Variable Cost per Unit
  <span class="ccm-f-label">Contribution margin ratio (percentage)</span>
  CM Ratio = Contribution Margin &divide; Revenue &times; 100
  <span class="ccm-f-label">Break-even point (in units)</span>
  Break-even Units = Fixed Costs &divide; CM per Unit
</div>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Total contribution margin</strong> tells you how many dollars a product line, month, or campaign contributed.</li>
  <li><strong>Contribution margin per unit</strong> tells you what each sale adds, which is what you need for pricing and break-even math.</li>
  <li><strong>The contribution margin ratio</strong> tells you what share of every revenue dollar is left. It&rsquo;s the easiest way to compare products or campaigns of different sizes. <a href="https://www.xero.com/us/guides/contribution-margin-ratio/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Xero&rsquo;s guide to the contribution margin ratio</a> walks through more examples.</li>
</ul>

<h2 id="steps">How to Calculate Contribution Margin, Step by Step</h2>
<p>Here is how to calculate contribution margin for any product or service, using a simple example. You only need two numbers to start: revenue and variable costs.</p>
<ol class="ccm-steps">
  <li><strong>Find your revenue.</strong> A product sells for $50, and you sold 1,000 units: $50,000 in revenue.</li>
  <li><strong>List every variable cost.</strong> Materials, labor, shipping, and payment fees come to $20 per unit: $20,000 in total.</li>
  <li><strong>Subtract.</strong> $50,000 &minus; $20,000 = <strong>$30,000 total contribution margin</strong>, or $30 per unit.</li>
  <li><strong>Divide by revenue for the ratio.</strong> $30,000 &divide; $50,000 = <strong>60%</strong>. Sixty cents of every dollar is left for fixed costs and profit.</li>
  <li><strong>Use it for break-even.</strong> With $12,000 in fixed costs, you break even at $12,000 &divide; $30 = <strong>400 units</strong>. Every unit after that adds $30 of profit, so 1,000 units produce $18,000 in profit.</li>
</ol>
<p>That is the textbook way to calculate contribution margin. It works cleanly for products because the variable costs are easy to see. Marketing is harder, because the costs that decide a campaign&rsquo;s profit live in different systems. More on that below.</p>

<h2 id="campaign">How to Calculate Contribution Margin for a Marketing Campaign</h2>
<p>Most guides stop at products and units. But if you spend money on ads or buy leads, the question that matters most is whether each <strong>campaign</strong> made money. That is where contribution margin gets tricky, and where ad dashboards quietly mislead.</p>
<p>To calculate contribution margin for a campaign, use the same formula with campaign-specific inputs. When you calculate contribution margin this way, the result is the profit a campaign really produced:</p>
<ul>
  <li><strong>Revenue</strong> is the revenue the campaign actually closed, minus refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks. It is not the conversion value an ad platform reports.</li>
  <li><strong>Variable costs</strong> are everything that rose because of that campaign:
    <ul>
      <li>the cost to deliver the work it won</li>
      <li>ad spend</li>
      <li>lead vendor and partner payouts</li>
      <li>payment and financing fees</li>
      <li>sales commissions</li>
      <li>compliance costs on bought leads</li>
    </ul>
  </li>
</ul>
<p>Some teams call the result &ldquo;contribution margin after marketing.&rdquo; The <a href="https://corporatefinanceinstitute.com/resources/accounting/contribution-margin-after-marketing-cmam/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">Corporate Finance Institute</a> describes it as contribution margin after deducting variable and marketing costs. Whatever you call it, it is the number that tells you whether to spend more. <a href="https://martech.org/contribution-margin-tell-marketing-makes-cents/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">MarTech makes the same case</a> for judging marketing on contribution margin rather than revenue.</p>

<h2 id="compare">Contribution Margin vs. Gross Margin vs. ROAS</h2>
<p>These three numbers get mixed up constantly. Here is how they differ:</p>
<div class="ccm-table-wrap">
<table>
  <thead><tr><th>Metric</th><th>Formula</th><th>What it answers</th><th>What it leaves out</th></tr></thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td><strong>Contribution margin</strong></td><td>Revenue &minus; all variable costs</td><td>Did this sale or campaign make money after the costs it created?</td><td>Fixed costs (by design)</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Gross margin</strong></td><td>(Revenue &minus; cost of goods sold) &divide; revenue</td><td>How profitable is what we sell, before selling costs?</td><td>Marketing, commissions, and other variable selling costs</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>ROAS</strong></td><td>Revenue &divide; ad spend</td><td>How much revenue did each ad dollar bring in?</td><td>Cost to deliver, partner payouts, refunds, fees, and profit</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>
</div>
<p>That is why businesses that calculate contribution margin per campaign make better budget calls than those that stop at ROAS. <a href="https://www.hubspot.com/glossary/return-on-ad-spend" target="_blank" rel="noopener">HubSpot defines ROAS</a> as revenue for every dollar spent on advertising, a useful speed check but not a profit number. If you want a marketing-specific profit metric, see our guide to <a href="https://alloceraintelligence.com/net-marketing-contribution/">net marketing contribution</a>, which is closely related and built for measuring marketing&rsquo;s profit impact.</p>

<h2 id="mistakes">Five Mistakes to Avoid When You Calculate Contribution Margin</h2>
<p>Most errors come from the inputs, not the formula. Watch for these five whenever you calculate contribution margin:</p>
<h3>1. Using reported revenue instead of revenue you kept</h3>
<p>Refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks often arrive weeks after the sale. If you calculate contribution margin on the revenue number from the day of the sale, you overstate it every time.</p>
<h3>2. Counting refunds twice</h3>
<p>Subtract refunds and chargebacks from revenue <em>or</em> count them as a cost, never both. Double counting is one of the most common spreadsheet errors. In the example above it would turn a $42,600 contribution margin into a $5,400 loss on paper.</p>
<h3>3. Stopping at ad spend</h3>
<p>Ad spend is only one variable cost of a campaign. Lead vendor and partner payouts, financing and payment fees, commissions, and the cost to deliver the work all belong in the calculation.</p>
<h3>4. Measuring the channel instead of the campaign</h3>
<p>&ldquo;Paid social ran a 25% contribution margin&rdquo; can hide one campaign at 45% and another losing money. Budget moves campaign by campaign, so that is the level to measure.</p>
<h3>5. Giving ads credit for revenue they didn&rsquo;t earn</h3>
<p>Referral and word-of-mouth customers often get attributed to whatever ad they clicked last. That inflates the campaign&rsquo;s revenue, and its contribution margin along with it.</p>
<p>More on hidden costs: <a href="https://alloceraintelligence.com/chargebacks-marketing-cost/">how chargebacks inflate true marketing cost</a> and <a href="https://alloceraintelligence.com/financing-fees-home-services/">how financing fees eat home services margin</a>. For a deeper look at the ROAS gap, see <a href="https://alloceraintelligence.com/roas-looks-good-campaigns-lose-money/">why ROAS can look good while campaigns lose money</a>.</p>

<h2 id="good">What Is a Good Contribution Margin?</h2>
<p>There is no single &ldquo;good&rdquo; number, because it depends on your fixed costs. A business with low overhead can thrive on a lower ratio. A business with a large team and office needs a higher one. The test is simple: your total contribution margin has to be bigger than your fixed costs, or you are losing money overall.</p>
<p>What the ratio means is straightforward. A <strong>40% contribution margin</strong> means 40 cents of every revenue dollar is left after variable costs. A <strong>30% contribution margin</strong> leaves 30 cents. Whether that is &ldquo;good&rdquo; depends on whether those cents cover your overhead with room to spare, and on whether the number is rising or falling over time.</p>
<p>For marketing campaigns, the most useful comparison is campaign against campaign. Once you calculate contribution margin for each one, the budget decisions get much clearer. Your highest-margin campaigns deserve more budget; the ones near zero deserve a hard look. That comparison only works if every campaign is measured the same way.</p>

<h2 id="faq">Contribution Margin FAQs</h2>
<details>
  <summary>How do you calculate contribution margin?</summary>
  <p>Subtract total variable costs from revenue. For example, $50,000 in revenue minus $20,000 in variable costs is a $30,000 contribution margin. Divide by revenue to get the ratio: $30,000 &divide; $50,000 = 60%.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>How do you calculate contribution margin per unit?</summary>
  <p>Subtract the variable cost per unit from the selling price per unit. A product that sells for $50 with $20 of variable cost has a $30 contribution margin per unit. Divide your fixed costs by that number to find your break-even point in units.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>How do you calculate the contribution margin ratio?</summary>
  <p>Divide contribution margin by revenue and multiply by 100. The ratio shows what share of every revenue dollar is left after variable costs. It&rsquo;s the best way to compare products or campaigns of different sizes.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>What is the difference between contribution margin and gross margin?</summary>
  <p>Gross margin subtracts only the cost of goods sold. Contribution margin subtracts all variable costs, including selling and marketing costs that rise with each sale. That makes contribution margin the better number for deciding where to spend.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>What does a 40% contribution margin mean?</summary>
  <p>It means 40 cents of every revenue dollar is left after variable costs, available to cover fixed costs and produce profit. Whether that is enough depends on your fixed costs.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>Is a 30% contribution margin good?</summary>
  <p>It can be, depending on your overhead. A 30% ratio leaves 30 cents per revenue dollar. If that covers your fixed costs with room to spare and it is holding steady or rising, it is healthy for your business.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>Can I calculate contribution margin in Excel or Google Sheets?</summary>
  <p>Yes. Put revenue in cell B1 and total variable costs in B2. Contribution margin is <code>=B1-B2</code>, and the ratio is <code>=(B1-B2)/B1</code> formatted as a percentage. The formula is easy. The hard part is keeping the inputs current when refunds, payouts, and fees live in different systems.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>What costs should I include when I calculate contribution margin for ads?</summary>
  <p>Include every cost that rose because of the campaign: ad spend, lead vendor and partner payouts, the cost to deliver the work it won, payment and financing fees, sales commissions, and compliance costs on bought leads. Leave out fixed overhead like rent and salaries. Subtract refunds and chargebacks from revenue.</p>
</details>
<details>
  <summary>Do refunds and chargebacks affect contribution margin?</summary>
  <p>Yes. They reduce the revenue you actually kept. Subtract them from revenue once, never again as a cost, and tie them back to the campaign that produced the original sale.</p>
</details>
