Net Marketing Contribution: The Formula That Shows What Your Marketing Really Earns

Net marketing contribution is the profit your marketing actually produces after you pay for what you sold and everything it cost to sell it. It is the most important number most businesses never see, because their ad dashboards were never built to show it.

Key takeaways

  • Net marketing contribution (NMC) = revenue × gross margin % − marketing and sales expenses.

What Is Net Marketing Contribution?

Net marketing contribution (NMC) measures how much profit your marketing generates after two things are paid for: the cost of delivering what you sold, and the cost of marketing and selling it. What is left over is marketing’s real contribution to the business. That is the money available to cover overhead and turn into profit.

The metric comes from Roger J. Best’s marketing textbook, Market-Based Management, published by Pearson. The book’s own site walks through the same idea with a public-company example in its article on measuring the profit impact of a marketing budget.

The idea behind it is simple. Marketing should be judged on the profit it creates, not the revenue it touches.

Most businesses still judge campaigns on revenue-based numbers like return on ad spend (ROAS) and cost per lead. Those numbers are not wrong. They just leave out the costs that decide whether a campaign actually made money. That gap is where marketing budgets quietly leak, and most owners never know it is happening.

The Net Marketing Contribution Formula

Here is the formula, plus the three related numbers that make it useful day to day.

Net marketing contribution NMC = Revenue × Gross Margin % − Marketing & Sales Expenses Same thing, written another way NMC = Revenue − Cost to Deliver − Marketing & Sales Expenses Marketing return on sales (NMC as a % of revenue) Marketing ROS = NMC ÷ Revenue × 100 Marketing return on investment Marketing ROI = NMC ÷ Marketing & Sales Expenses × 100 Breakeven ROAS Breakeven ROAS = 1 ÷ Gross Margin %

Each piece in plain English:

  • Revenue is the money you actually kept. Use closed revenue after refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks, not the number an ad platform reports as a “conversion value.”
  • Gross margin % is the share of each sale left after the direct cost of delivering it: labor, materials, or product cost.
  • Marketing and sales expenses are everything it took to win the business. That means ad spend plus lead vendor and partner payouts, agency fees, call tracking, sales commissions, and any processing or financing fees tied to the sale.

Breakeven ROAS is the number most teams have never calculated, and it changes how you read every dashboard. It is the minimum ROAS a campaign needs just to cover its ad spend at your gross margin:

3.33×Breakeven ROAS at a 30% gross margin
2.5×Breakeven ROAS at a 40% gross margin
2.0×Breakeven ROAS at a 50% gross margin

If your gross margin is 40%, every campaign below a 2.5 ROAS is losing money on ad spend alone, whatever color the dashboard shows. And that is before any other marketing cost is counted.

How to Calculate Net Marketing Contribution, Step by Step

You can calculate NMC for a whole business, a product line, or a single campaign. For budget decisions, the campaign level is where it matters most, because that is where money actually moves.

  1. Start with the revenue you kept. Take the closed revenue the campaign produced, then subtract refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks.
  2. Apply your gross margin. Multiply that revenue by your gross margin %, or subtract the direct cost of delivering the work. This is your gross profit.
  3. Add up every marketing and sales cost. Include ad spend, lead vendor or partner payouts, agency fees, call tracking, and any fees tied to the sale. Leave out overhead like rent. Net marketing contribution is what pays for that.
  4. Subtract. Gross profit minus marketing and sales expenses is your NMC.
  5. Turn it into ratios so you can compare. Divide by revenue for marketing ROS, and by marketing expenses for marketing ROI. Now a $5,000 campaign and a $50,000 campaign can be compared fairly.

Net Marketing Contribution vs. ROAS

Return on ad spend is revenue divided by ad spend. HubSpot, the Corporate Finance Institute, and Amazon Ads all define it the same way: revenue generated for every dollar spent on advertising. It is a fast, useful signal. It is just not a profit number.

ROAS leaves out four things that NMC includes:

  • The cost to deliver what you sold. A $1,500 job that costs $1,200 to complete is a very different sale from one that costs $600. ROAS treats them the same.
  • Every marketing cost beyond ad spend. Partner payouts, agency fees, and call tracking never show up in ROAS.
  • Money that comes back later. Refunds, cancellations, and chargebacks still count as revenue in most ROAS reports.
  • Deals that never close. Ad platforms often count a lead or a booked call as a conversion, whether or not it ever turned into paid revenue.
ROAS measures revenue. Net marketing contribution measures profit.

Why Most Businesses Never See Their Real Number

If this number is so useful, why isn’t everyone tracking it? Because the numbers it needs live in different systems that were never designed to talk to each other.

  • Your ad platforms know spend, clicks, and the conversions they can see.
  • Your CRM knows which leads became deals and what those deals were worth.
  • Your call tracking knows which calls came in and which ones turned into jobs.
  • Your payment processor knows about refunds and chargebacks, often weeks later.
  • Your lead partners’ invoices know what you paid for each lead.

None of these tools is doing anything wrong. Each one was built to do its own job well. Nobody built them to reconcile each other. So the true profit of a campaign has to be pieced together by hand, usually in a spreadsheet, usually after the budget has already been spent. The costs that slip through most often:

Refunds and cancellations

They arrive after the sale, so the ad platform already counted the revenue.

Chargebacks

Disputed payments quietly reverse revenue your reports still show.

Partner and lead payouts

What you pay brokers or lead vendors rarely appears next to ad spend.

Deals that never close

A “conversion” on the dashboard is not the same as money in the bank.

Referral revenue credited to ads

Word-of-mouth customers get counted as paid wins, which flatters the wrong campaigns.

Edited close dates

When CRM dates get changed by hand, revenue shifts into the wrong period and the wrong campaign.

Any one of these can turn a campaign that looks profitable into one that loses money. Together, they explain why so many businesses feel like marketing is working while the bank account says otherwise. Our contribution margin calculation guide walks through how to account for each cost by hand.

Where Net Marketing Contribution Matters Most

Net marketing contribution matters most in businesses where profit per sale varies widely, and where leads are bought, routed, or closed long after the click. That describes most service and lead-driven businesses:

  • Home services: a full system replacement and a small repair can come from the same ad at very different margins. See true cost per booked job for HVAC and plumbing.
  • Personal injury law: case types differ enormously in value, and many leads never become signed cases. See cost per signed case.
  • Senior living and healthcare: one move-in or admission can be worth far more than another, and sales cycles run long. See true cost per move-in.
  • Insurance, mortgage, and solar: bought leads, partner payouts, and financing fees sit between the ad and the profit. See how financing fees eat margin.

In every one of these, a single blended ROAS hides which campaigns bring in high-margin work and which ones fill the pipeline with work that looks busy but earns little.

Net Marketing Contribution FAQs

How do I calculate my net marketing contribution?

Multiply the revenue you kept by your gross margin %, then subtract your marketing and sales expenses. For example, $57,000 in revenue at a 45% gross margin is $25,650 in gross profit. Subtract $21,200 in marketing and sales costs and your net marketing contribution is $4,450.

What is marketing contribution?

Marketing contribution is the share of profit your marketing creates. Net marketing contribution makes it precise: it is gross profit minus marketing and sales expenses, the amount your marketing adds toward covering overhead and producing profit.

What is the difference between net marketing contribution and contribution margin?

Contribution margin is revenue minus all variable costs, usually measured for a product or a whole business (MarTech has a good explainer on contribution margin for marketers). Net marketing contribution focuses on marketing: gross profit minus marketing and sales expenses. At the campaign level, both answer the same question: did this spend make money after the costs it created?

What is net marketing contribution percentage?

Expressing net marketing contribution as a percentage of revenue is called marketing return on sales (marketing ROS): NMC ÷ revenue × 100. It lets you compare campaigns of very different sizes on equal footing.

What is a good net marketing contribution?

There is no universal benchmark, because gross margins and overhead differ by business. A positive net marketing contribution means a campaign covers its own costs and adds profit. The useful questions are whether it covers your share of overhead and whether it is rising or falling over time.

Is ROAS still worth tracking?

Yes. ROAS is a fast signal of how efficiently ads turn into revenue. Pair it with your breakeven ROAS and your net marketing contribution, so a strong-looking ROAS never hides a campaign that is losing money.

Do I need software to track net marketing contribution?

Not for a handful of campaigns. A spreadsheet works. It gets hard to keep up when your data is spread across ad platforms, a CRM, call tracking, and payment tools, and when refunds keep arriving after the fact. That is the work CDAI automates every night.
